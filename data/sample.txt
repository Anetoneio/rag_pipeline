Retrieval-Augmented Generation (RAG)

Retrieval-Augmented Generation, or RAG, is a technique that combines
information retrieval with text generation. Instead of relying only on
what a language model memorized during training, RAG first searches an
external knowledge source for relevant information, then feeds that
information to the model as context before it generates an answer.

Why RAG matters

Large language models have two weaknesses that RAG directly addresses.
First, their knowledge is frozen at training time, so they cannot
answer questions about new or private documents without being
retrained. Second, they can hallucinate: producing fluent but
factually wrong answers when they are uncertain. By grounding the
model's answer in retrieved text, RAG reduces hallucination and lets
the system answer questions about documents it has never seen during
training, such as a company's internal PDFs.

The stages of a RAG pipeline

A RAG pipeline typically has six stages. Ingestion loads raw documents
from disk, such as PDFs, text files, or Markdown files. Chunking splits
each document into smaller, overlapping pieces so that each piece is
short enough to embed and specific enough to be individually relevant.
Embedding converts each chunk into a numerical vector that captures its
meaning. Indexing stores those vectors in a vector store that supports
fast similarity search. Retrieval takes a user's question, embeds it
the same way, and finds the chunks whose vectors are closest to the
question's vector. Generation passes the retrieved chunks along with
the original question to a language model, which produces a final
answer grounded in that context.

Chunk size and overlap

Choosing a chunk size is a trade-off. Very small chunks are precise but
may lack surrounding context, which can make retrieved text hard for
the model to interpret. Very large chunks carry more context but dilute
relevance, since a chunk that only partially matches the question may
still be retrieved because part of it scores well. A moderate chunk
size, combined with a modest overlap between consecutive chunks,
usually gives a good balance: overlap ensures that a sentence spanning
a chunk boundary is not split away from its context in every chunk.

Vector similarity search

Once chunks are embedded, retrieval reduces to a nearest neighbor
search: given a query vector, find the stored vectors closest to it
under some distance metric, most commonly cosine similarity. Vector
stores such as FAISS, Chroma, and many managed vector databases exist
specifically to make this search fast, even across millions of vectors.

Limitations of RAG

RAG is not a complete solution to hallucination. If the retriever
fails to find the relevant chunk, the model may still answer
incorrectly, sometimes confidently. RAG systems are also sensitive to
how documents are chunked: a poor chunking strategy can split
important information across chunks in a way that makes it hard to
retrieve as a coherent unit. For these reasons, evaluating and tuning
the retrieval step is often just as important as choosing a good
language model for generation.
